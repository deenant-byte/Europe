1. Europe's map: the first mental model

Europe is a continent and a historical-cultural region, not a single country or a uniform culture. Its societies have been shaped by geography, migration, trade, religion, empires, war, and political cooperation. Countries that are close together can have different languages and institutions because national identities developed over centuries.

Useful regional groupings

  • Western Europe: often includes France, Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg.
  • Southern Europe: often includes Italy, Spain, Portugal, and Greece.
  • Northern Europe: commonly includes Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, and Iceland; the UK and Ireland are classified differently depending on context.
  • Central Europe: often includes Austria, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, and Slovenia.
  • Eastern Europe and the Baltics: classifications vary; examples include Ukraine, Moldova, Belarus, Russia's European region, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania.
  • The Balkans / Southeast Europe: includes countries such as Albania, Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Kosovo, and often Croatia, Slovenia, Bulgaria, and Greece depending on the definition.

Key principle: regional labels are useful shortcuts, not fixed scientific categories. A person may identify strongly with a city, region, language community, country, or several of these at once.

2. Europe, the EU, the eurozone, Schengen, and NATO

TermWhat it meansWhy it matters
EuropeA continent and broad historical-geographical region.Includes EU and non-EU countries.
European Union (EU)A political and economic union of 27 member states.Coordinates policies and operates a single market, among other shared arrangements.
EurozoneEU countries that use the euro as their currency. Membership changes over time; Bulgaria adopted the euro in January 2026.EU membership does not automatically mean a country uses the euro. Poland uses the złoty; Sweden uses the krona.
Schengen AreaA group of countries that generally remove routine checks at internal borders.It includes non-EU countries such as Switzerland and Norway. It is not the same as the EU or a right for every foreign national to live and work anywhere.
NATOA collective-defence alliance that includes European countries, the US, Canada, and others.It is not an EU institution and is not exclusively European.
Council of EuropeA separate international organization focused on human rights, democracy, and rule of law.It is distinct from both the EU and the European Council.

EU institutions in one minute

  • European Commission: proposes much EU legislation, implements policies, and monitors compliance with EU law.
  • European Parliament: directly elected by EU citizens; shares legislative and budgetary powers with the Council of the EU.
  • European Council: national leaders set broad political direction and priorities.
  • Council of the European Union: national ministers meet by policy area and co-legislate with Parliament.
  • Court of Justice of the EU: interprets EU law.

Remember the distinction between the European Council (leaders) and the Council of the European Union (ministers). They are different bodies.

3. Geography and climate shape daily life

Major physical features

  • The Alps: influence transport routes, tourism, water supplies, and settlement across several countries.
  • The Rhine: a major inland transport corridor linking industrial and commercial regions with ports.
  • The Danube: connects Central and Southeastern Europe and passes through or along many countries.
  • The Pyrenees: form a significant mountain barrier between France and the Iberian Peninsula.
  • The Scandinavian Mountains: help shape Norway's steep western landscape and local climate patterns.

Broad climate patterns

RegionCommon patternPossible everyday effects
Atlantic-facing Western EuropeOften mild, cloudy, and changeable.Layered clothing and frequent rain.
Mediterranean SouthOften hot, dry summers and milder, wetter winters.Heat adaptation, outdoor life, and water management.
Inland Central/Eastern EuropeMore continental conditions in many places.Greater seasonal temperature contrasts.
Nordic and Arctic areasCold winters and major seasonal daylight variation.Heating needs and changes in outdoor routines.
Alpine areasStrong elevation-related variation.Snow, mountain transport, and microclimates.

At high northern latitudes, summer days can be extremely long and winter days very short. The North Atlantic circulation, prevailing winds, latitude, and geography all influence Europe's climate; no single ocean current explains it by itself.

Connection to Indonesia: European rivers often link inland cities to ports and cross national borders. Indonesia's archipelagic geography makes maritime and inter-island logistics especially important. Different geography creates different economic and infrastructure challenges.

4. History: why modern Europe looks the way it does

Use this timeline as a framework. Later lessons can expand each era in detail.

Late antiquity and Middle AgesWestern Roman imperial rule ended; Byzantine power continued in the east; kingdoms, cities, and religious institutions developed.Regional identities, universities, church institutions, and legal customs.
Renaissance and ReformationRenewed engagement with classical learning; religious reform and conflict.Art, education, church-state relations, and religious diversity.
Enlightenment and revolutionsDebates on reason, rights, sovereignty, and government; French Revolution.Modern citizenship, constitutionalism, nationalism, and political ideologies.
Industrialization and empireFactories, urbanization, railways, global trade, and colonial expansion.Modern class structures, wealth gaps, infrastructure, and contested colonial legacies.

World War IINazism, fascism, the Holocaust, occupation, and immense destruction.Human-rights frameworks, remembrance, reconstruction, and security policy.
Cold WarEurope divided between Western-aligned states and Soviet-dominated communist states.Different political and economic experiences across the continent.

Do not treat this as a story of inevitable progress. European history includes intellectual achievement and democratic development, but also slavery, colonial violence, religious persecution, genocide, and aggressive nationalism.

5. Culture, food, and everyday life

National cultures are not personality tests. Use broad patterns as questions to explore, not assumptions about individuals.

Food as history

  • Italy: regional cuisines vary considerably between north and south; pasta and coffee traditions are culturally significant, but everyday practices differ.
  • Spain: tapas, rice dishes, cured ham, and strong regional cuisines reflect local agriculture, trade, and history, including the influence of Al-Andalus.
  • Germany: bread diversity, regional sausages, beer traditions, and local food festivals are important, but modern diets are varied.
  • France: bread, cheese, regional cooking, and gastronomic institutions have become part of national cultural identity.
  • Portugal: seafood, bacalhau, pastries, and maritime connections feature prominently.
  • Nordic countries: fish, rye, preservation traditions, and contemporary reinventions reflect climate and local resources.
  • Central and Eastern Europe: soups, dumplings, fermented foods, pastries, and hearty dishes vary widely across countries and regions.

Social life and etiquette

  • Expect differences in punctuality, directness, formality, personal space, meal times, and attitudes toward privacy.
  • Friendship often develops through repeated shared activities: classes, clubs, sports, work, or invitations.
  • Humor may rely on irony, understatement, teasing, or self-deprecation; tone and familiarity matter.
  • Regional identity may be as important as national identity for some people.
  • Ask how a person experiences their culture rather than assuming they embody a national stereotype.

Useful campus questions

  • “Is there a food from your region that visitors rarely know about?”
  • “What do people usually do on weekends where you grew up?”
  • “Is your city or region culturally different from the rest of your country?”
  • “What's a tradition you enjoy, even if you don't follow every tradition?”

6. Football: sport, identity, and economics

Football is more than a game: clubs can represent cities, neighborhoods, industries, social groups, family traditions, or regional identities. Never assume every person follows it, but understanding the basics can make conversation easier.

Country / regionCompetitions and examplesUseful context
GermanyBundesliga; Bayern Munich, Borussia Dortmund, SchalkeThe 50+1 rule generally limits outside investors' control of clubs, with details and exceptions. Supporter culture and stadium attendance are prominent.
SpainLa Liga; Real Madrid, Barcelona, Atlético MadridEl Clásico is a major sporting rivalry with political and regional associations, though its meaning varies by individual.
ItalySerie A; Juventus, AC Milan, Inter, Roma, NapoliLocal rivalries, tactical traditions, and the north–south divide can appear in football discussions.
EnglandPremier League and the wider football pyramidPromotion and relegation connect leagues; clubs often have deep local roots alongside global commercial brands.
NetherlandsEredivisie; Ajax and othersDutch football is associated internationally with Total Football and youth development.
France and PortugalLigue 1; Primeira LigaYouth academies, player development, scouting, and transfers are important themes.

Essential vocabulary

  • League: competition in which teams accumulate points over a season.
  • Promotion/relegation: teams move between divisions based on results.
  • Derby: match between local or regional rivals.
  • Transfer window: period when clubs can register eligible player transfers.
  • Academy: a club's youth-development system.
  • Ultras: organized supporter groups, particularly associated with intense matchday displays; groups vary greatly.
  • Champions League: UEFA's leading European club competition; separate from domestic leagues.

Conversation starter: “Which club do you support, and is it a family tradition or your own choice?” If you don't know the club, ask what makes its rivalry or fan culture distinctive.

7. Government and public policy

Common political systems

  • Parliamentary system: the government depends on parliamentary support. Germany, Italy, and the UK are examples, though their constitutional arrangements differ.
  • Semi-presidential system: an elected president and a prime minister share executive roles. France is a prominent example.
  • Constitutional monarchy: a monarch is head of state under constitutional rules; examples include Spain, Sweden, Denmark, and the Netherlands.
  • Federalism: constitutional powers are divided between national and regional governments. Germany, Switzerland, and Austria are examples.
  • Direct democracy: citizens vote directly on certain questions; Switzerland is especially known for this.

Why is university “free” in Germany?

Public universities in many German states generally do not charge ordinary tuition fees for many consecutive-degree programs, but students usually pay semester contributions. Living expenses, health insurance, housing, and other costs remain. Rules differ by state, institution, program, and student category; some non-EU students face tuition charges in certain states or programs.

This illustrates a broader principle: a public service can be tax-funded without being cost-free to the individual in every respect. Eligibility, funding, quality, and access all matter.

Comparing welfare systems fairly

When comparing healthcare, education, parental leave, pensions, unemployment support, or housing, ask:

  1. Who is eligible?
  2. How is it funded—taxes, social insurance, fees, or a mix?
  3. What is the level of service and how easy is access?
  4. What are the trade-offs, such as tax levels, waiting times, or public spending priorities?
  5. Does the rule differ by region, citizenship, residency, or employment status?

“Free” and “expensive” are incomplete descriptions without information about eligibility, funding, and actual outcomes.

8. Economics and living standards

Europe contains wealthy, high-productivity economies as well as countries and regions with lower incomes and different development challenges. Avoid assuming that all European countries offer the same standard of living.

  • GDP: value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period.
  • GDP per capita: GDP divided by population; it is not the same as the typical person's income.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): adjusts comparisons for differences in local prices.
  • Productivity: output produced per unit of input, often measured per hour worked.
  • Disposable income: income remaining after direct taxes and including relevant cash benefits.

Broad economic profiles

  • Germany: manufacturing, engineering, exports, vocational training, and a large network of specialized firms.
  • France: a mix of private enterprise, public services, state involvement in strategic sectors, agriculture, aerospace, luxury goods, and tourism.
  • Italy: manufacturing districts, design, fashion, food, tourism, and many small and medium-sized enterprises; regional economic differences are significant.
  • Spain: services, tourism, manufacturing, agriculture, and persistent debates about youth employment and housing.
  • Netherlands: trade, logistics, agriculture technology, and international business.
  • Nordic economies: varied combinations of competitive private sectors, high taxes, extensive public services, and strong labor-market institutions.
  • Central and Eastern Europe: industrial integration, foreign investment, services, and economic convergence, alongside varied national challenges.

Two countries can have similar average output per person but different rent, taxes, public services, inequality, wages, or working hours. Compare several indicators rather than ranking countries by one number.

9. Languages, religion, and ideas

Language families

  • Romance: French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian; descended largely from spoken Latin.
  • Germanic: English, German, Dutch, Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Icelandic, and others.
  • Slavic: Polish, Czech, Slovak, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Croatian, and others.
  • Baltic: Lithuanian and Latvian.
  • Celtic: Irish, Welsh, Scottish Gaelic, and Breton, with distinct histories and current vitality.
  • Uralic: Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian, and Sámi languages, among others.
  • Basque: a language isolate; it is not generally classified as Indo-European.

A shared language family does not mean two languages are mutually understandable. Language also carries identity: Catalan, Basque, Welsh, and other regional languages can be tied to histories of autonomy, suppression, revival, and cultural pride.

Religion and secularism

European religious history includes Catholicism, Protestantism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Judaism, Islam, and many forms of nonreligion. Religious affiliation, observance, and public influence vary by country and generation. The region's history includes both religious institutions' contributions to education and art and episodes of persecution, antisemitism, forced conversion, and conflict.

Ideas that shaped politics

  • Liberalism: traditions emphasizing individual rights, freedoms, and limits on power.
  • Conservatism: diverse traditions emphasizing continuity, institutions, and gradual change.
  • Socialism: broad traditions seeking greater social or economic equality through different forms of collective action or ownership.
  • Social democracy: generally combines a market economy with redistribution, social insurance, and public services.
  • Nationalism: the idea that a nation should have political significance or self-determination; it has taken both emancipatory and exclusionary forms.
  • Secularism: different arrangements for the relationship between religion and public institutions; France and other European countries interpret it differently.

10. Campus conversation toolkit

Five questions that invite real conversation

  1. “Do you identify more with your country, your region, or your city?”
  2. “How does university funding work where you grew up?”
  3. “Which football club do you support, and why?”
  4. “What's a dish from your region that foreigners usually don't know?”
  5. “Is there a historical event that still influences politics in your country?”

How to use cultural knowledge without stereotyping

  • Start with the individual's experience; use national context as a follow-up, not a conclusion.
  • Ask open questions rather than testing someone on their own country.
  • When politics or history is sensitive, ask whether they want to discuss it and listen before debating.
  • It is fine to say, “I don't know much about that—can you explain?” Curiosity is better than pretending to be an expert.
  • Connect Europe to your own experience. For example, compare cross-border European rivers and railways with Indonesia's inter-island transport and linguistic diversity.

11. Quick glossary

Federalism
Constitutional division of authority between national and regional governments.
Secularism
Principles and arrangements governing the relationship between religion and public institutions.
Proportional representation
An electoral approach in which seats are allocated broadly in proportion to votes.
Welfare state
A system in which public institutions provide or finance social protection and services.
Coalition government
A government formed by two or more political parties.
Single market
An integrated market designed to allow the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people within the EU framework, subject to applicable rules.
Schengen
A border-control arrangement among participating countries, distinct from EU membership.
Regional autonomy
Self-government powers exercised by a region within a state.
Purchasing power parity
A method for comparing purchasing power by accounting for price-level differences.
Promotion and relegation
Movement between football divisions based on sporting results.
